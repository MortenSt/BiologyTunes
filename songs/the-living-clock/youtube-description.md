The Living Clock: A Song of Degree Days – Forensic Entomology | BiologyTunes

How can a fly tell investigators when someone died? This dark-folk ballad follows the blowfly's life cycle, from the first egg to the newly emerged adult, and shows how forensic entomologists read it as a living clock powered by heat.

🪰 WHAT THE SONG COVERS
• The call: after death, cells break down (autolysis) and decay releases volatile compounds that blowflies detect within minutes to hours. Species prefer different places: Lucilia sericata sunny, urban sites; Calliphora vicina shade and cooler weather; C. vomitoria rural woodland.
• Degree days: insects can't regulate their body temperature, so development runs on heat. Entomologists add up degree-days (ADD) above a lower threshold; below it, development pauses.
• Egg and instars: eggs are laid in wounds and natural openings such as the eyes. The larva passes through three instars, and the third is by far the largest and does most of the feeding.
• Reading the clock: matching the oldest stage found to the heat accumulated at the scene gives a minimum post-mortem interval (PMImin).
• Leaving to pupate: post-feeding larvae usually wander off and burrow into soil, so investigators search the ground nearby. The cold-tolerant Protophormia terraenovae often pupates on or right beside the remains.
• Emergence: the adult pushes out of the puparium and expands and hardens its wings before flying. Empty puparia show that at least one full cycle has passed.
